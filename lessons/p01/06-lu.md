## The multipliers are a matrix

Each elimination step subtracts $\ell_{ik}$ times row $k$ from row $i$. Collect every multiplier into a unit lower triangular matrix

$$L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & & \\ \ell_{21} & 1 & \\ \ell_{31} & \ell_{32} & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$

and call the triangular result $U$. Undoing the subtractions means adding the multiples back, which is exactly multiplication by $L$. So without pivoting $A = LU$, and with row swaps recorded in a permutation matrix $P$,

$$PA = LU .$$

To solve $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$, multiply by $P$ and split the work in two:

$$L\mathbf y = P\mathbf b \quad (\text{forward substitution}), \qquad U\mathbf x = \mathbf y \quad (\text{back substitution}).$$

## Why factor once and solve many times

Factoring costs $\tfrac{2}{3}n^3$ operations. Each pair of triangular solves costs about $2n^2$. When the same matrix meets many right-hand sides, as in an implicit simulator that solves with the same Jacobian at every step, or a Kalman filter reusing a covariance factor, you pay the cube once.

For $n = 500$ and 100 right-hand sides, re-running elimination costs $100 \times \tfrac{2}{3}(500)^3 = 8.33\times10^{9}$ operations. Factoring once and solving 100 times costs $\tfrac{2}{3}(500)^3 + 100\times 2(500)^2 = 1.33\times 10^{8}$. That is 62.5 times less work.

## Worked example: a 2 × 2 factorisation

Factor $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 6 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and solve $A\mathbf x = (10, 12)$.

1. Pivot: $|6| > |4|$, so $P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ and $PA = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 3 \\ 4 & 3\end{pmatrix}$.
2. Multiplier $\ell_{21} = 4/6 = 2/3$. Row 2 becomes $(0,\ 3 - \tfrac23\cdot 3) = (0,\ 1)$.
3. So $L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2/3 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ and $U = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 3 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$. Check: $LU = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 3 \\ 4 & 3\end{pmatrix} = PA$.
4. $P\mathbf b = (12, 10)$. Forward: $y_1 = 12$, $y_2 = 10 - \tfrac23 \cdot 12 = 2$.
5. Back: $x_2 = 2$, $x_1 = (12 - 3\cdot 2)/6 = 1$. Check: $A(1, 2) = (10, 12)$.

## Code it from scratch

:::code file=lu.py
```python
import time
import numpy as np

def lu(A):
    U = np.array(A, dtype=float)
    n = U.shape[0]
    L, perm = np.eye(n), np.arange(n)
    for k in range(n - 1):
        p = k + np.argmax(np.abs(U[k:, k]))
        if p != k:
            U[[k, p], k:] = U[[p, k], k:]
            L[[k, p], :k] = L[[p, k], :k]
            perm[[k, p]] = perm[[p, k]]
        for i in range(k + 1, n):
            L[i, k] = U[i, k] / U[k, k]
            U[i, k:] -= L[i, k] * U[k, k:]
    P = np.eye(n)[perm]
    return P, L, U

def lu_solve(P, L, U, b):
    y = P @ np.asarray(b, dtype=float)
    n = len(y)
    for i in range(n):                        # forward, L has a unit diagonal
        y[i] -= L[i, :i] @ y[:i]
    x = np.zeros(n)
    for i in range(n - 1, -1, -1):            # back substitution
        x[i] = (y[i] - U[i, i + 1:] @ x[i + 1:]) / U[i, i]
    return x

if __name__ == "__main__":
    P, L, U = lu([[4, 3], [6, 3]])
    assert np.allclose(L, [[1, 0], [2/3, 1]]) and np.allclose(U, [[6, 3], [0, 1]])
    assert np.allclose(lu_solve(P, L, U, [10, 12]), [1, 2])
    rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
    A = rng.standard_normal((120, 120))
    P, L, U = lu(A)
    assert np.allclose(P @ A, L @ U, atol=1e-12)
    B = rng.standard_normal((120, 30))
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    X = np.column_stack([lu_solve(P, L, U, B[:, j]) for j in range(30)])
    t1 = time.perf_counter()
    for j in range(30):
        lu(A)                                  # re-eliminating every time
    t2 = time.perf_counter()
    assert np.allclose(A @ X, B, atol=1e-9)
    print(f"30 solves with one factorisation: {1e3*(t1-t0):.1f} ms; 30 re-eliminations: {1e3*(t2-t1):.1f} ms")
```
:::end

## Exercises

:::exercise
Factor $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 5\end{pmatrix}$ with partial pivoting.
:::solution
1. $|4| > |2|$, so swap: $PA = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 5 \\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}$.
2. $\ell_{21} = 2/4 = 0.5$; row 2 becomes $(0,\ 1 - 2.5) = (0,\ -1.5)$.
3. $L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, $U = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 5 \\ 0 & -1.5\end{pmatrix}$.
:::end

:::exercise
A simulator solves with the same 2000 × 2000 matrix at 1000 time steps. Estimate the operation counts with and without reusing the LU factors.
:::solution
1. Re-eliminating: $1000 \times \tfrac23 (2000)^3 = 5.33\times 10^{12}$.
2. Reusing: $\tfrac23(2000)^3 + 1000\times 2(2000)^2 = 5.33\times10^9 + 8\times 10^9 = 1.33\times 10^{10}$.
3. Reusing is about 400 times cheaper.
:::end
